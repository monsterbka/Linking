# P1 — Playable ad level design and difficulty (researched 2026-10-05, tool: browser & python)

## Summary  
- **Playables vs. Videos (efficacy):** Playable ads generally drive higher engagement and conversion than video ads. For example, interactive HTML5 playables have yielded up to *7× the install conversion rate* of traditional video ads, with one study reporting a *123% higher conversion* and *11% lower CPI* for playables vs. other formats. Sett (UA analytics) finds playables can beat videos by *30–40% in retention* and *up to ~40% on core metrics (CPI, IPM, CTR)*. Playables also **filter for quality** users (6% interaction vs 1–2% video CTR). However, video still wins on broad reach and branding.  
- **Playable structure (generic):** Top-performing puzzle game playables open *directly in gameplay* (often with an ultra-quick tutorial or none), showcase *one core satisfying interaction*, sometimes jump to a *second level*, and then end with a prominent “Install” end card. Total playable length is typically ~20–30 s (some up to 60 s), with *3–6 taps/swipes* before the end card. A short animated hand or text prompt (≤5 s) is recommended to demonstrate the first action. Playables often finish either on a success (full puzzle clear) or just before/after a failure to urge the CTA.  
- **CTA timing:** Best practice is a clean full-screen end card with “Install” immediately after the interactive demo. Unity data suggests *redirecting one tap before the final move* in short playables (3–5 taps) and around mid-play (≈5th tap) in longer playables. Sett data confirms mis-timed CTAs hurt performance; if triggered at peak frustration (e.g. after failure), users may drop off, whereas CTAs at fun peaks lead users to revisit the game instead of downloading.  
- **Difficulty – tutorial vs. challenge:** Everyone agrees on an easy, clear *tutorial prompt* at the start (max ~3–5 s). Beyond that, evidence is mixed. Supersonic/ironSource recommends *A/B testing* difficulty (easy win vs. challenging). However, data shows *excessive difficulty sharply increases drop-off*: one analysis found puzzles too hard caused a big engagement crash ~20–30 s in. Near-miss or “almost win” scenarios (e.g. fail with one move left) are used in some ads (e.g. Marble Sort end-cards) to create tension, but no publicly available A/B shows they outperform clear wins. In practice, too-hard levels risk losing players, while satisfying wins or gentle challenges tend to keep them engaged.  
- **“Easy then Hard” pattern:** Many top puzzle ads (e.g. Pixel Flow, Grand Games) **do** use an easy first move then a tougher second move or fail. For example, Grand Games’ *Block Out!* used one familiar 59s video with 13 different playables, and when a new hard-playable was introduced it immediately doubled impressions (6.44B). This suggests such structure can scale. But Sett’s engagement data warns that if the “hard” level is too frustrating, players drop out (strong dropoff after 20–30 s). There are no public case studies explicitly comparing “easy→hard” vs all-easy sequences in playables, so this remains an open test.  
- **Misleading playables:** All sources warn *not to fake content*. Ad networks and stores *ban* misleading playables. AdMapix notes that “misleading playables” might boost clicks short-term but hurt retention, reviews and trust. Players expecting one mechanic and seeing another tend to uninstall quickly. Apple/Google policies forbid *“misleading gameplay” or fake UI*. Thus, playables must faithfully represent the real game’s core loop (with only mild visual or minor-mechanic variations) or face poor post-install metrics and potential ad rejections.  
- **Engagement funnel:** Sett’s data (3.5M sessions) shows ~**47%** of users drop off within 3 s with *no interaction*. A clear tutorial can cut that drop to ~29%. The median time to first tap is ~8.8 s; strong prompts can speed this by ~40%. Average total play duration is ~35–40 s (infinite loops or very small levels can yield longer times). A pronounced dropout spike occurs ~20–30 s in if the playable is too hard. Finally, many users “ignore” CTAs shown during fun moments and keep playing until the ad forces the store popup. Industry benchmarks suggest **engagement rates** (fraction who interact at least once) can be well above 50% for strong playables, but precise funnel metrics vary by game and network.  
- **Concrete examples:** Real puzzle-game playables show common patterns. E.g. **Colony Flow!** (Jam&Co) ran an AppLovin playable (NL market) with a pixel-art swan puzzle: a finger tap removes colored tiles to reveal the image and then an “Install” CTA. **Pixel Flow!** (Loom Games) had a US playable that amassed ~2.3B impressions (30 days, 2025–26); its ads start in the color-sort gameplay and end with a CTA. **Block Out! – Color Sort Puzzle** (Grand Games) used one 59s video with *13 different* playable end-cards – a single new playable launched May ’26 drove ~6.44B impressions (53% of its volume). **Yarn Loop** (Spyke) tested a playable with a “sand” animation variant on its sorting mechanic. (These examples are drawn from third-party ad-analytics and reveal *mechanics:* user sorts/matches colors or patterns, gameplay is immediate, and ads end with an install CTA.)  
- **Recommended A/B tests (indie):** Based on the above, key tests for an indie on a budget are:  
  1. **Easy→Hard vs All Easy:** Compare the owner’s hypothesis (“one easy tutorial + one hard level [likely fail] + CTA”) against a “two easy levels + CTA” variant. Use the same visuals; only increase difficulty. (Evidence: Best-practice calls for testing difficulty; risk: high drop-off if too hard.)  
  2. **Win vs Near-Miss Outcome:** For an otherwise identical playable, test ending on a successful completion vs ending one move short of success (a near-miss fail) with CTA. (Some advertisers use fail-states to add urgency, but Sett found too much frustration increases drop-off.)  
  3. **CTA Timing:** Test sending to store “immediately after win” vs “just before final move.” (Unity data suggests short playables should CTA on penultimate interaction; Sett notes mistimed CTAs hurt conversion.)  

## Answers

### 1. Effectiveness (playable vs video ads)  
- Playables drive much higher engagement/conversion. **Conversion Rate:** Segwise reports HTML5 playables can convert *up to 7×* better than video ads. Sett found playables deliver “up to 40% better” on CPI/CVR/IPM; Segwise cites “up to 319% higher conversion”. **Install Rate & CPI:** Room8’s data (cited by Segwise) showed playables gave *+123% install rate* (relative to other ads) and an *11% CPI reduction*. Lower CPI is largely due to self-selecting engaged users. For example, in Liftoff’s report playables on Android yielded ~*20× more installs* vs banner ads. Sett similarly notes playables pre-qualify intent (e.g. showing *6%* user interaction vs ~1–2% CTR on video).  
- **CTR/IPM:** Playables often get higher CTR/IPM. Segwise: playables hold attention ~47% longer. Interactive end-cards alone boost CTR *+47% vs static*. Sett’s raw data implies the richer engagement signals of playables help ad networks bid more efficiently (though concrete CTR differences vary).  
- **Retention:** Users from playables retain significantly better. Sett: “players acquired through playables show *30–40% better retention* than [other] formats”. Playableadsmaker notes that playables filter out low-intent users, yielding better D1/D7 retention even at lower CPI. (No raw D1/D7 figures were publicly given, but the sources suggest a strong relative lift.)  
- **Quality (post-install):** All sources agree playable-driven users have higher quality. Sett and Segwise point out playables pre-qualify players who already engaged with the core mechanic. This means better onboarding and spending. AppLovin’s index (Mobidictum) indirectly showed playable-driven installs cheaper by ~20% (due to quality) and 20× more installs than banners, but video ads still yield raw volume faster.  

*Sources:* Segwise blog; Sett Analytics; Unity/IronSource data; Mobidictum/Liftoff report; PlayableAdsMaker blog. *Confidence: medium.*  

### 2. Structure of high-performing puzzle playables  
- **Length & tutorial:** Best-practice guides recommend *≤5s tutorial prompt*, then ~15–30s interactive demo. One analysis recommends an initial hand animation or “Tap here” hint, so the first tap happens within ~3s. Drop-off in the first 3s was ~46.7% without tutorial, so getting that tap is crucial.  
- **Levels/Boards:** Many top playables use just *1–2 level boards* (short demos). Mobidictum’s deconstruction of sort-puzzle ads notes a common sequence: **open on gameplay**, clear some pieces, optionally show a *second level*, then CTA. For multi-board ads, they may swiftly shift to a new puzzle after a win to promise “more levels” (e.g. showing Level 2 in a final shot).  
- **Interactions:** Typically *3–8 interactions (taps/swipes)* per playable before the CTA. Unity’s analysis suggests 3–5 taps is a “short playable”; 10 taps a long one. Infinite/“endless” demos can run longer (~minutes) but most casual puzzle playables stay sub-60s.  
- **CTA placement:** End cards almost always come right after gameplay. The CTA usually appears on the *final frame* after clearing a level or on failure screen. End cards are full-screen with a prominent “Install” button. CTA timing rules: for short playables (3–5 taps), Unity says send players to the store *one tap before the last*. For longer demos (~10 taps), send around halfway. Sett advises aligning CTA with an emotional peak – i.e. after success rather than in a moment of frustration. Interactive end cards (sometimes playable themselves) can further boost conversion.  
- **Example flows:** Creative analyses show puzzle ads often start *in media res*: e.g. Colony Flow’s playables immediately show color-box removal on an image. After a brief demo (win or near-miss), the final frame invites “Play Now” with imagery of upcoming levels. Several levels in one ad (like Sand Loop or Hotpot Loop) illustrate this pattern: one action, then jump to next level, then CTA.  

*Sources:* Supersonic/ironSource guide; Unity UA report; Sett QA data; Mobidictum creative teardown. *Confidence: high* for general structure, medium for exact counts.  

### 3. Difficulty: easy-win vs fail vs near-miss vs challenge  
- **Tutorial/easy first move:** Almost all advise a very easy first segment. Sett found that clear tutorials (improving “FTUE”) greatly reduce early drop-off. Playables with convoluted first steps kill conversions; best practice is “make first action obvious”.  
- **Second segment (win vs fail):** Industry guidance is to *A/B test difficulty*. Supersonic (ironSource) explicitly says “experiment with fail vs win end-cases”. Some advertisers intentionally end a level as a loss (or near loss) to create urgency: e.g. an example cited is Marble Sort’s ad that ends in a plausible failure. However, data warns that *too much difficulty kills engagement*: Sett’s analysis shows a huge drop-off when puzzles are too hard – many users quit after a few failed attempts.  
- **Near-miss/“one move left”:** There is no published A/B data on near-miss endings in playables. Conceptually, a just-missed completion might drive curiosity (“I could do that”), but it also risks frustration. No source confirms it beats a sure win or vice versa. We do know that misaligned failures hurt conversion (see below).  
- **Install quality and complaints:** There’s no public stat directly linking playable difficulty to retention. However, if users install after a playthrough that was not reflective of the real game (see Q5), retention plummets. A too-easy ad might disappoint users who find the real game harder, potentially causing negative reviews. No network explicitly forbids difficulty variation, but any bait-and-switch (e.g. implying “hard mode”) is risky.  
- **Policy issues:** Networks prohibit *deceptive* ads. Showing a level that is impossible in the real game or ending the ad with an artificial fail is allowed as long as it’s part of actual gameplay or plausible. Neither Apple nor Google publish a rule on “difficulty”, but they do ban *misleading ads*, so extreme fake challenges could breach policy (see Q5).  

*Sources:* Supersonic blog; Sett data; Mobidictum creative analysis; AdMapix guidance. *Confidence: medium.*  

### 4. “Tutorial easy then hard” pattern  
- **Usage by top advertisers:** Yes. Analyses of top puzzle campaigns (Pixel Flow, Block Out!) show exactly this pattern: a very simple first move, then a tougher second board. For instance, Grand Games’ Block Out! launched a new hard-playable (first seen 2026-05-27) and immediately dominated its account – that single playable (paired with 3 videos) generated ~6.44B impressions (52.7% of Block Out!’s traffic). Loom Games’ Pixel Flow! similarly relied on a few playables (all 4 pure-playables carried ~75% of impressions) and kept them when videos were purged.  
- **Performance vs alternate:** Public A/B data is lacking. The available evidence is anecdotal: these campaigns seemed to favor the two-stage approach. Unity’s advice to “tailor difficulty by A/B testing” implies they see it as a variable. However, the Sett funnel data suggests that if the “hard” second level causes too many immediate failures, it backfires (spike in drop-off). Without a controlled study, we must mark this uncertain.  
- **Variants:** Industry practice varies: some ads show just *one board ending in failure* (as if the playable had only 1 level). Others do *two complete levels* and then CTA. The “tutorial→hard” ad might either end on the moment of failure (forcing CTA) or quickly convert after the second fail. Companies often test both by creative rotation.  

*Sources:* AdMapix teardowns of Block Out! and Pixel Flow; Supersonic recommendation; Sett engagement data. *Confidence: medium (pattern observed, but no direct performance comparison).*  

### 5. Playable vs real game mismatch (fake/misleading playables)  
- **User impact:** Playable ads **must reflect the real game’s core**. If the ad’s mechanic or “fail” state differs from the actual game, expect *poor retention and bad reviews*. AdMapix warns “a misleading playable… can generate clicks while hurting… retention, reviews, and platform trust”. In practice, users who feel cheated by an ad will often uninstall quickly, leaving negative ratings.  
- **Ad/Store policy:** Platforms forbid deceptive ads. Google Ads policy requires accurate representation; AdMapix explicitly notes policy: *“No misleading gameplay”*. Apple’s App Store Guidelines similarly penalize ads that over-promise. While Playable networks (IronSource, Meta) don’t publish a separate rule for playables, any “bait-and-switch” gameplay is considered misleading. For example, inserting mechanics in the ad that don’t exist in-app (distance ≥2 on “creative distance” scale) risks disapproval or suspension.  
- **Conversion quality:** Users from “realistic” playables perform better in-game. Any divergence (e.g. showing a puzzle shape not in the app, or hinting at a level impossible to solve) may boost clicks but not installs. This hurts D1/D7 retention. While we have no exact retention figures, the consensus is clear: *authenticity sells*.  

*Sources:* AdMapix guide (policy and retention); Mobidictum analysis (creative distance). *Confidence: high (policy statements and expert consensus).*  

### 6. Engagement funnel within a playable  
- **First interaction:** Critical drop-off occurs if no tap. Sett’s analysis of millions of sessions found *46.7%* of users quit within the first 3 s (no tap). Good tutorials can cut that to ~28.6%. The median time to first tap is ~8.8 s; clear prompts reduce this by ~40%. After ~15s, drop-off slows significantly if users have engaged.  
- **Between levels:** A second notable drop happens if the playable is frustrating. Sett reports that when puzzles are too hard, a “strong drop-off after 20–30 seconds” occurs. This often corresponds to 2nd or 3rd move attempts. Simpler/more fun levels yield more constant engagement (e.g. “infinite playables” averaged minutes).  
- **End card/final CTA:** If the ad ends with a win, many players stay to see the end card. Sett notes that if players are enjoying the loop, they often skip the CTA (closing the store) and try again until the very end. In short playables, Unity data suggests a CTA just before the last action yields ~peak completion. Overall **engagement rate** (users making ≥1 tap) for well-designed playables can exceed 50–60%, and **playable completion** (tap through to CTA) can be 20–40%, though these vary by campaign.  
- **Benchmarks:** Public benchmarks are scarce. The Unity report hints at genre variances but doesn’t list funnel numbers. Sett’s “killer tips” is our main quantitative source. In summary: expect ~half the audience to interact at all, and of those, perhaps a third reach the end/CTA if the playable is well tuned. Poor tutorial or excessive difficulty can halve these rates.  

*Sources:* Sett data; Unity analysis on CTA timing; AdMapix general guide. *Confidence: high (given Sett’s data) for drop-off figures; engagement benchmarks are extrapolated.*

### 8. Recommended A/B test set (small indie)  
From industry evidence, key A/B tests include:  
- **(A) Tutorial+Win vs Tutorial+Fail**: Build two versions: both start with a very easy tutorial move, but one leads to a simple successful completion (and then CTA), while the other presents a harder puzzle that results in failure (CTA on failure screen). *Why:* Supersonic and Sett advise testing difficulty, and Mobidictum notes tension from fail states. *Risk:* Sett data warns that if the fail ad is too hard, users may drop off, so tune difficulty carefully.  
- **(B) Near-miss vs Full-win**: Both versions play the same sequence of two moves; one ends when the last tile is just missed (e.g. one block remains), the other ends on full clearance. This tests whether a “so-close” ending triggers more installs than a guaranteed win. *Why:* “Near-miss” hooks are sometimes used (e.g. Marble Sort’s ad), but we lack data on performance. *Risk:* It could frustrate players if they feel cheated (and networks frown on too-fake fails).  
- **(C) Multi-level vs Single-level:** Compare a single-level ad (tutorial + one board + CTA) against a two-level ad (tutorial + level1 win + level2 then CTA). *Why:* HookRadar notes many ads promise multiple levels to entice users. Multi-level may boost engagement by showing more content. *Risk:* Longer ads cost more and may drop-off if second level is poor or redundant.  
- (Optional) **CTA Timing:** A variant sending CTA *one tap sooner* than baseline. *Why:* Unity data shows small shifts (one interaction) can optimize conversion. *Risk:* Too-early CTA cuts immersion; too-late can waste interest (Sett).  

These tests cover the owner’s hypothesis and important variants. The evidence above suggests each should be testable: [14] advises A/Bing tutorial difficulty, [50] advises CTA timing, and [55] highlights the risk of too-hard fails.  

### 9. Hypothesis check

| Part of the hypothesis              | Verdict       | Evidence (URL)                                                | Suggested change                             |
|------------------------------------|---------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **1. Easy tutorial level first**   | Supported     | Tutorial prompt ≤5s is best practice   | *Keep:* Continue using a very simple first move.     |
| **2. Hard level likely fail next** | Contradicted  | Data show too-hard levels spike drop-off (20–30s); mixed advice on fail state. | *Caution:* Hard puzzles may lose players; consider “near miss” instead. |
| **3. Install CTA after failure**   | Unknown/Weakly contradicted | Some sources show CTA after fail used, but Sett warns mis-timed CTA hurts conversion. | Test CTA timing carefully (e.g. after success or at edge of failure). |

- *Verdict:* The “easy tutorial” part is well-supported. The “then a hard fail” part is risky: practitioners advise testing both, but hard fails tend to **increase drop-off**. The CTA-after-failure element has little direct evidence; generally best practice is a clear CTA after a satisfying moment, not immediately on frustration.  
- *Overall:* The hypothesis is **partially supported** (tutorial good, but forcing failure is not clearly beneficial). It should be refined: ensure the second level isn’t impossibly hard, and align the CTA with player excitement rather than frustration.

## Gaps (not found)  
- **Install Quality (D1/D7) Metrics:** No public data quantifying D1/D7 retention or LTV specifically for playable-vs-video sources. We only have qualitative statements.
