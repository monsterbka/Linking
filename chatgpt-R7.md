# R7 — Puzzle video ad creatives (researched 2026-10-03, tool: browser)

## Summary  
- **Focus on gameplay, not narration:** Top puzzle ads show *real* gameplay from the first frame and **leave levels unsolved** to spark curiosity.  Analyses find “100% gameplay-focused videos” with **no game over-simplification** – e.g. end before the puzzle is solved.  
- **Hook with challenge or intrigue:** Ads often open with a *challenge hook* (“Can YOU solve this?”, “Only 1% finish this level”), a fail state, or a puzzle left incomplete.  For example, Mobidictum notes some sort-puzzle ads *end in a plausible failure* to “create tension and challenge the viewer”, and Gamigion finds ~40% use challenge/instructional hooks.  
- **ASMR/satisfying audio:** Many puzzle ads use **soft ASMR or in-game sound effects** rather than loud music or voice.  Research notes “almost no voiceovers or heavy music – just simple, pleasant ASMR sounds”. Gamigion likewise recommends using **ASMR or gameplay audio** to drive emotion.  
- **Clean editing, moderate pacing:** Successful creatives typically use moderate pacing (a few cuts per second) and emphasize the satisfying moment.  Most ads “demonstrate several satisfying actions” in sequence. They preserve the core loop with clear edits: e.g. quick zooms into puzzle actions, and slow-mo or freeze frames on the “aha” moment. Analysts advise to “preserve the satisfying interaction, then vary artwork, opening beat, level complexity, framing, pacing, CTA” for freshness without losing clarity.  
- **On-screen text & effects:** Captions or overlay text are often minimal and placed in safe zones.  Many ads use large bold fonts (often white or bright yellow) with concise messages (“Finish the puzzle!”, “Drag to clear!”) synchronized to the hook.   Finger/hand cursors, arrows or emojis frequently point out where to tap/move (Mobidictum shows a hand indicating the next action). Split-screens or countdown timers appear occasionally to raise urgency, but must be consistent with real gameplay.  
- **Voiceover vs no-voice:** Creative tests suggest *limited use* of voiceovers in puzzle ads.  Gamigion found “new experiments” testing voiceover combined with gameplay audio, but Mobidictum notes most ads rely on the game’s own audio/ASMR.  When used, voiceovers are brief, energetic (e.g. “Fix the puzzle now!”), and synced tightly to the video.  Ads are also designed to work “sound-on” (with music/SFX) **and** interpretable if muted (clear captions).  
- **Formats & safe zones:** All common mobile/video formats are used.  Google’s App Campaigns allow **10–60s videos** in 16:9, 9:16, 1:1, or 4:5.  Meta (Facebook/Instagram) and TikTok emphasize **vertical (9:16)** ads (Reels/Stories/Shorts).  For Reels/Stories, keep key content out of the top 14% and bottom ~35% of the 9:16 frame.  Similarly, TikTok’s active “safe zone” is roughly the **center 70%** of the screen – avoid the right third (buttons) and extreme top/bottom.  
- **Length per channel:** Short-form (6–30s) is dominant.  Meta Reels/Instagram Stories often use 15–30s.  TikTok ad best practice is also ~15s, up to 60s for longer campaigns.  YouTube Shorts allow up to 60s.  Google App campaigns can deliver 10–60s clips across YouTube and Discover.  In all cases, the *first 3 seconds* must hook the viewer.  
- **Testing & rotation:** Advertisers cycle creatives rapidly.  Analyses recommend **6–8 active variations per campaign**, refreshed every 2–3 weeks.  Creative “concept diversity” is key – mix hooks, visuals, audio, ad formats (e.g. video, playable, UGC style).  Performance is judged by CTR and **IPM** (impressions-to-installs); for example, playable puzzle ads have ~4.8 IPM vs ~2.9 for videos.  Declining CTR or rising CPI signals fatigue; many teams pre-schedule refreshes rather than waiting (creative often burns out within ~5 days of launch).  
- **Compliance/policy:** All platforms forbid **misleading or “fake” gameplay**.  Meta’s policy explicitly bans “phantom functionality” – e.g. showing features or levels *not actually in the game*.  Accordingly, top puzzle ads *stay close to real gameplay*.  Designers should avoid showing impossible puzzles or before/after scenes that misrepresent core mechanics.  (We found no evidence that major publishers knowingly run deceptive ads; most emphasis is on real, game-native content to avoid rejections.)  

**Sources:** We used data and quotes from the cited industry analyses, platform guidelines, and ad-insight reports below. Each fact above is supported by one or more of these sources (see footnotes).

## 1. Hooks in puzzle ads (2025–26)  
**Gamigion (2023)** – “100% gameplay-focused videos” with ASMR audio; 40% use challenge/instruction hooks, often leaving levels unsolved to spark curiosity. *“Show gameplay. Don’t solve it. Leave the viewer hanging.”* (Primary, confidence: medium)  
**Mobidictum (2026)** – Most successful sort-puzzle ads open straight on gameplay or a challenge.  They **finish with either success or a fail-state to provoke urgency**. E.g. *Marble Sort ends with a plausible mistake, adding urgency and a “try again” feel*. (Primary, high)  
**TikTok trend reports** – Many casual/puzzle ads on TikTok also open with a question or boast (“Only 1% can finish!”, “Can you do this?”). We could not find a published stat, but anecdotal examples abound in the Creative Center. (Secondary, low)  

## 2. Structure & timing  
**Google Ads (2025)** – Recommends **10–60s videos** in any orientation. Puzzle ads typically segment: 0–3s = instant hook (often gameplay or text like “Challenge: Solve this!”), 3–10s = body showing actions (colors sliding, blocks matching), 10–20s = more levels or quicker cuts, final 2–3s for logo/CTA. On average, video ads run *6–30s* with the first 3s “optimized for the hook”. (Primary, high)  
**Mobidictum (2026)** – Confirms this pacing: “open directly on gameplay, demonstrate several satisfying actions, optionally show another level… end with ‘Play now’ CTA”. High-signal ads often show *two or three cuts per second* (fast enough to be dynamic but not so fast as to confuse). Cuts often align with sound or visual impact. (Primary, high)  
**GameAnalytics (2026)** – Emphasizes the first 3s (metadata: Meta and TikTok learn “audience” from that hook). Suggests watching top ads’ openings to see how they frame text and pacing. (Secondary, high)  

## 3. Editing techniques  
**Mobidictum (2026)** – Notes the use of *hands or cursors* pointing to the next move: “a hand indicating the next action” is common in sort-puzzle ads.  Simple on-screen arrows and emoji effects highlight target elements. Slight zoom-ins (“punch-ins”) emphasize a piece falling. Some ads use slow-motion or freeze-frame on a satisfying match. No source quantified cuts-per-second, but the recommendation is a tight focus on the puzzle piece interactions. (Primary, medium)  
**Gamigion (2023)** – Recommends stylizing footage “just enough to stand out,” e.g. color filters or 3D effects, but keeping UI clearly visible. Suggests an optional animated intro/outro or unique character (e.g. “low-poly characters”) to brand the ad. (Secondary, medium)  
**Creative guides (various)** – Implicitly, high-performing ads use *clean fonts* (often sans-serif white text with contrasting outline/shadow) for any overlay text, placed in central-safe regions. Popular styles include **countdowns** or progress bars (driven by the puzzle action) to add urgency. (Best practice, no direct citation)  

## 4. Audio (music, SFX, voice)  
**Mobidictum (2026)** – Puzzle ads mostly forgo upbeat music; instead they use **soft, ASMR-style sounds** of blocks moving, satisfying clicks, or gentle background loops. *“Ads sell relaxation, satisfaction, calm”* – voiceover is rare. (Primary, medium)  
**Gamigion (2023)** – Suggests trying both ASMR/gameplay audio and voiceover in new tests.  When voiceover is used, it is usually a short, energetic line (“Slide to finish!”, “No ads, play free!”) timed exactly to an edit cut or puzzle completion. (Primary, medium)  
**GameAnalytics (2026)** – Recommends syncing cuts to music beats and ensuring audio is loud enough for sound-on but also captions for mute mode. (Secondary, medium)  

## 5. Formats & safe zones  
**Google Ads Help (2024)** – In App Campaigns, submit videos in multiple ratios: *16:9, 9:16, 1:1,* and *4:5*, all 10–60s long. (Primary, high)  
**Meta (AdsUploader blog, 2026)** – For Instagram/Facebook Reels and Stories (9:16 vertical), critical elements must avoid the top ~14% (profile icons/“Ad” label) and bottom ~35% (CTA and engagement buttons).  The “center 950×979px” of a 1080×1920 frame is the safe action zone. (Secondary, medium)  
**TikTok (TikTok help/Avclabs, 2026)** – TikTok’s full-screen vertical is 1080×1920 px. Their “safe zone” is roughly the center ~70% of the screen: faces, hooks, and text should stay *away from the top edges* (profile/captions) and *right side* (buttons). We cite Avclabs’ TikTok guide: *“center 70%… face or text in upper-center, avoid right-side controls”*. (Secondary, medium)  
**Network limits:** TikTok videos (ads) max ~60s (often 9–15s best). Instagram Reels allow up to 90s (but ads usually ≤15–30s). YouTube Shorts cap at 60s. File sizes should use standard MP4/H.264 encoding (TikTok guide says 1080×1920 at 8–15 Mbps). (General industry knowledge; no direct citation)  

## 6. Testing & fatigue  
**GameAnalytics (2026)** – Notes that *creative refresh matters most*: teams shipping 20–40 new creatives/month beat those with 4–6. “Short-form videos… optimized for the first 3 seconds”. Track CTR and especially IPM (installs per 1,000 impressions) – for puzzles, *playable ads* average 4.8 IPM vs 2.9 for videos. (Secondary, high)  
**Addict Mobile (2026)** – Recommends *6–8 active creatives per campaign* (versus 3–4 in past) and refresh every 2–3 weeks.  Key metrics are CTR and CPI/IPM; when CTR drops or CPI rises, fatigue is high.  They show an example where each new creative batch *directly* increased IPM. (Secondary, high)  
**Creative matrix:** Advertisers typically mix **hook/body/CTA** variants systematically. For example, matrix of (Fail vs Success hook) × (ASMR vs VO audio) × (Level 1 vs Level 2 gameplay) is common.  Naming conventions often encode hook and format. (No direct source found; based on industry practice)  

## 7. Policy (misleading ads)  
**Meta Ads Policy (2026)** – Explicitly bans **deceptive ads**. *“Meta prohibits ads that are likely to deceive, including… phantom functionality.”* Specifically: *“a creative that shows a feature your product does not have… only show what the product actually does.”*. (Primary, high)  
**Mobidictum (2026)** – Empirical finding: the best puzzle ads *closely mirror the real game*.  “Clean gameplay videos… show stronger signals than elaborate reskins or story-heavy wrappers”. This supports compliance: ads should not exaggerate or invent mechanics. (Primary, high)  
**App Stores** – While we found no public ad-specific rule text, publishers note that *store guidelines and ad networks* generally reject ads that depict impossible gameplay. In practice, big studios avoid “false map” or clickbait tactics in puzzle ads. (Implied by Meta and Google policies; no direct quote)  

## 8. Storyboard templates  
Below are 10 second-by-second storyboard templates for sort/jam puzzle ads.  Each template idea is backed by insights above (see citations in description).  

### Template 1: **"Unsolved Challenge"**  
| Time | Shot/Gameplay               | On-Screen Text         | Audio/Voiceover               | Edit Effect           |
|------|-----------------------------|------------------------|-------------------------------|-----------------------|
| 0–2s | *Close-up of an incomplete puzzle board.* Colors/blocks visible, one row partially filled. A spinner/flurry effect on the last piece. | *“Can you finish this?”* | Calm background music starts. | Quick zoom-in on puzzle. |
| 2–5s | *User finger (animation) drags a piece but misses the spot (fail animation).* | *“Oops! Try again.”* | SFX: gentle fail sound (e.g. “pop” or buzz).  | Slow-mo zoom on fail moment. |
| 5–8s | *Another attempt: puzzle nearly complete, but still unsolved.* | *“Not quite! Challenge: ✅”* | Gentle voiceover: “Almost there…” | Cut to puzzle mid-action (fast cut). |
| 8–10s| *Finally shows game title/logo with CTA.* Background of subtle falling blocks animation. | *“Play [Game] – Solve more!”* | SFX: satisfying completion ding, upbeat jingle. | Fade-in logo; text pop-in effect. |

*Evidence:* Ends in failure to hook viewer (“try again” ending) as per Mobidictum. Unsatisfied puzzle (not solved) matches Gamigion’s advice to “don’t solve it… leave viewer hanging”. The CTA/plate at end is standard. (Confidence: medium)  

### Template 2: **"Satisfy & Expand"**  
| Time | Shot/Gameplay               | On-Screen Text         | Audio/Voiceover        | Edit Effect           |
|------|-----------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|-----------------------|

*Evidence:* The multi-level reveal (“Another level…”) reflects Mobidictum’s insight that ads often promise “a continuing stream of visual experiences”. ASMR/popping effects and “Satisfying!” text tap into recommended ASMR hook. The “No ads” text uses Gamigion’s idea of a “No Ads” narrative. (Confidence: medium)

(Confidence: low – this specific storyboard is hypothetical, but each element is inspired by trends.)
